Workflow-based navigation

Creating navigation that highlights core use-cases.

Roles
Lead product designer, UX engineer
Tools
Figma, Storybook, Angular, HTML, CSS
Team
1 product designer/UX engineer, 2 developers
Timeline
Design: Nov 2023 – Apr 2024
Development: Apr – Jun 2024 (4 weeks)
Implementation: Jul – Aug 2024 (4 weeks)

Overview

Sourcescrub's customers are primarily analysts seeking difficult-to-obtain data on private companies for their investment banks and private equity firms. Sourcescrub provided access to complex database searches. These searches had many different possible workflows and data views which were difficult to discover and remember. Customer service teams spent large portions of their week training and retraining customers on these workflows.

Goal

We wanted to understand which workflows were most important to customers and make those the primary entry points in our main navigation. This would improve the customer experience and reduce the amount of training required by our teams.

User research

The first step was to understand how our users were most likely to interact with the app. We used a combination of what we already knew about usage from Pendo and Datadog, and combined it with which workflows required the most training or were least discoverable, based on input from our Customer Success team.

Then, we worked with our revenue teams to create a final list of workflow categories and specific workflows within each.

Our legacy page layout with the original navigation. Not much space for expansion.

UI design

Once we understood how the navigation should be grouped, we needed to find the best way to show all of the options.

I reviewed common patterns for navigation used by our competitors. Most used nested menus across the top, or in sidebars. Competitors were often organizing data like we did—in terms of the data structure, rather than user outcomes.

I expanded my search to other patterns, and found that Material 3 had rail-and-drawer conventions that focused on larger, simple categories represented by icons, and drawers with more options for specific workflows or information types. This fit our use-case perfectly, and was achievable by our front-end developers.

The streamlined vertical navigation rail prototype.

Maximized space

By moving navigation to the side, we could reduce the height of sticky headers and controls needed for workflows, giving more space for data.

Perfect for desktop

Our users are primarily on desktop, and this approach feels intuitive in that setting. The icon/category approach will also be easy to translate to mobile in the future.

Intuitive design

While the exact implementation isn't as common as other sidebars, Sidebar navigation is a convention almost as old as the internet itself.

Easily expandable

We can fairly easily add a few more large categories, and we can add a lot of workflows in the drawer. Not that we should, of course. Too many choices gets cluttered.

Implementation

The designs were completed, and documentation was written to demonstrate the intent of the interaction. I also created working Angular components in our UI-kit and Storybook to give them a solid foundation for refinement.

A detail I am particularly proud of is the animation delay on the drawer. A user must linger on an icon in the rail for a few milliseconds before the drawer opens. This keeps unintentional mouseovers from triggering the drawer.

Dynamic Header Controls

It was also important to me to update the overall page layout so that a user always has access to the main page controls. To this end, I created a header that reacts to the user's position in the page.

Context-aware header reacting smoothly as users scroll through dense financial tables.

Release and aftermath

We created an aggressive timeline for a phased release in collaboration with our Account managers and Customer success teams. They hand-picked vanguard customers for the first phase. We got immensely good feedback from this change and so proceeded with two more phases—each larger than the previous.

We anticipated a slight dip in NPS score, or increase in negative feedback, as large changes often have that sort of reaction, but the opposite occurred. We saw an increase in positive feedback, and NPS remained steadily high.

However, a small poll conducted through Pendo immediately after release showed respondents were a bit polarized on their opinions.

Overall customer response

Zero customers asked for the legacy UI to be turned back on for them after getting access to the new UI. Every customer directly surveyed said it was much easier to find what they were looking for.

Learnings

Change can be hard, but the care taken in building the navigation to match customer behaviors made the launch a great success. Implementation of this layout did pose some interesting challenges, as we had to preserve the old layout to some degree. We used Split.io to manage that behavior, but a lot of fundamentals had to be rewritten to support the shift in layout.

Next steps

While a clickable navigation is a great start, we also want users to have the opportunity to navigate the app using more free-form inputs, so the input in the top nav bar needs to be updated to support workflow behaviors. Additionally, we want to update the navigation to have a cleaner, more contemporary style once other style changes are completed in-app.
