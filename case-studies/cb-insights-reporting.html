Automated sector reports

Turning up the volume of information design output through automation.

Role
Lead Designer, Design Technologist
Tools
PowerPoint, Looker, Python, HTML, CSS, XML
Team
1 data engineer, 1 designer/UX engineer
Timeline
May 2022 – January 2023

Overview

CB Insights is a market intelligence platform with a distinct visual style. To solidify the perception of authority, they frequently share PowerPoint reports and data visualizations on LinkedIn. They wanted to vastly increase the number of reports they released.

Goal

CB Insights asked me to join their team in 2022 to take the number of reports they released from around 4 per year to 30+ by completely eliminating the most tedious and error-prone parts of the process: chart building and data transfer.

Automated report generation in action – transforming raw data queries into styled, editable PowerPoint decks.

Design the reports

The design approach was the first step of the process, where I pored over every previous report the teams had created manually to find obvious patterns. I also had specific design guidelines to follow:

  • CB Insights' distinctive digital-nostalgic colors and accent elements (new at the time)
  • Unique data visualization guidelines for label placement, etc.
  • A desire to keep the appearance as close to hand-crafted as possible
  • Specific table styles which rarely wrap
  • Alignment with digital app designs
  • Charts needed to be editable after export

All of these needed to be translated into PowerPoint's XML files and automated via Python.

Structure the data

I worked with a data scientist who ported cuts of the CB Insights database into Looker, an analytics platform. There, she and I worked to get the cuts into very simple formats which would work with PowerPoint's simple charting. The most complex charts used no more than two sets of axis labels for each axis. We determined an overall, reproducible structure, section-by-section, page-by-page which could be iterated over. Each report, regardless of content, would have the same overall structure.

Using Python, I wrote the first piece of the final software, which would use Looker's API to convert these cuts into Python Pandas data tables. From there, they could be manipulated into various shapes required by the data. Python Pandas allows simple data analysis and manipulation, necessary for converting it into PowerPoint formats.

The most complex charts were bar/line combos with different axis values.

Export the reports

Once I had the data structured, I was able to use Python-pptx and the ElementTree XML API to convert the Pandas data frames into PowerPoint pages and apply simple styles like colors, font-sizes etc.

The first reports run had 80% of the styles applied, which already saved a huge amount of work for our teams, but there were still many manual processes to go.

Automated report generation in PowerPoint with custom fonts, colors, and layout templates applied.

Technical refinements

Turning basic automation into production-quality reports required solving nuanced graphical layout challenges inside PowerPoint's rendering engine.

Collision avoidance: Dynamic leader-lines and smart nudging prevent label overlaps.

Vertical axes: Custom plot space calculation and contrast-aware color shifts.

Horizontal axes: Custom XML automation for quarters, YTD groupings, and time series.

Label placement

Labels took a large amount of refinement, as there were many complex charts with a lot of data. They could not overlap. To make this happen, I reverse-engineered how chart layouts worked in PowerPoint, and built a label-tracking system which calculated label size, determined position, and compared coordinates to detect collision.

If a label overlapped, it was nudged out of the overlap zone, and a leader-line applied if it was over a specific amount.

Vertical axes

Because placement of objects is calculated based on how much space exists in the plot, I reverse-engineered how value-axes are calculated in PowerPoint and modified the system to have a more visually-consistent output by providing allowances for labels.

This calculation also allowed for me to track if a label fell on top of a white background or colored bar and change its color value accordingly.

Horizontal axes

Most charts were time series, and many of them depicted quarters. Our team wanted these to be displayed as seen above. To achieve that, I created the display manually, then pulled apart the XML where those settings were saved and automated the creation of matching custom XML for these charts.

The primary challenge here was getting the style right for the YTD, since the number of quarters wasn't consistent run-to-run.

CB Insights more than quadrupled their report output due to this project. They were also able to create reports for sub-sectors and trending markets—something very costly to do before. Analysts and designers went from building 200+ pages per report to copying and editing 10.

Learnings

While I had experience automating in PowerPoint before this project, I learned a huge amount about how math and digital design are deeply intertwined, and how human touches can be replicated by deep pattern recognition and replication.

Ideas for next steps

Before departing the project, I had begun to explore the possibility of data analysis to automate the large portion of the executive summary. The executive summary was still very manual, as it required a lot of human input about the content and styles.

I believed analyzing the charts analysts chose would reveal patterns which could be used to automatically select candidates for the executive summary and style them correctly—leaving humans the much easier job of removing the pages that weren't appropriate.
